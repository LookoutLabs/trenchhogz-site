The Field Manual

Everything the War Office is willing to put in writing: how a turn works, who the pigs are, what the toys do, and the several ways a match can end. The alpha is in active tuning — the rules below are the contract; the exact numbers are the balance team's problem.

Section 1

The Basics

Trench Hogz is squad-versus-squad turn-based artillery war. You field a squad of pigs; so does the other side. Turns alternate until one side has no pigs left — or until the mode you're playing says otherwise.

One move, one action

On its turn a pig repositions, then spends its single action — fire, lob, heal, place a device, or commit to something it will regret. Then the other side goes.

Aim it yourself

Flat guns fly straight inside the sights and need a clear lane. Arcing weapons trade certainty for reach — set the angle, judge the power, clear the parapet, and accept what the wind thinks of your plan.

Friendly fire is always on

A grenade does not check dog-tags. Your own pigs are exactly as squishy inside a blast radius as the enemy's. This is policy, not a setting.

Terrain is a weapon

Trenches, parapets, wrecks and water shape every shot. High ground earns range; hard cover stops flat guns dead; the map edge and whatever lives in the water are both permanently out of patience.

Last herd standing

Eliminating the enemy squad wins in every mode — even ones with their own objective. A fallen pig goes out with a dignified squeak and a puff of smoke.

Quick Play

One tap from the title screen into a skirmish on your last settings — squad, map, rules, everything. The game remembers your favourite way to lose pigs.

Section 2

The Roster — ways to be a pig

Every class is a trade. The Grunt is the yardstick; everyone else swaps one axis for another — speed for armour, health for reach, firepower for the ability to keep everyone else alive.

The backbone

Grunt

Rifle, pot helmet, boundless naïve courage. Cheap and plentiful. You will lose a lot of these. That is the point.

The closer

Assault

Gets in close and ends conversations. Shotgun answer to a rifle question — the shortest distance between two trenches is an Assault who has decided.

Demolitions

Grenadier

Grenades, TNT, mines, questionable judgement. Rules the field by making whole stretches of it unwise to stand on, and lobs from outside every flat gun's reach.

The wall of ham

Heavy

Belt-fed machine gun and a bazooka, slow as a Sunday roast. Nothing pins a line down like the Heavy leaning on the trigger.

Field surgeon

Medic

Keeps your veterans breathing so you don't have to keep training fresh Grunts. Healing wins campaigns; the memorial board is the alternative.

Marksman

Sniper

Fragile, patient, and able to reach across the whole map to ruin one specific pig's afternoon — if the line of sight is honest.

Cloak & blade

Phantom

Cloaks, closes, and works quietly: a sleep dart to put a sentry down, a blade to make it permanent. Executing a sleeping enemy is called Goodnight, and the War Office does not ask questions.

The fifth team

The Dogs

A monoclass melee swarm with cyber-grafts, a spine gun, and a spare life apiece. Kennel names beside unit designations. They do not do subtle.

The sixth faction

The Tribals

Bows, spears and the only true arcs in the game — everything they loose goes up before it comes down, and it comes down where it pleases. Clan epithets, no two alike.

Section 4

Killstreaks

Do enough damage in one match and the War Office starts returning your calls. Streak rewards escalate — from information, to ordnance, to the end of the map.

UAV

Eyes overhead for a spell. A convenience in the open; under Fog of War, the difference between shooting and guessing.

Mortar Rack

A stonk on a position of your choosing. The wind, as ever, is a neutral party with opinions.

Guided Missile

Flown personally from a field tablet. One designation, one very attentive missile. The tablet accepts no responsibility for pilots who chase a moving target into their own trench.

The Nuke

A five-beat cinematic, a bunker with room for the quick, and a door that does not reopen. Riding it out sealed inside is an achievement. Standing outside is a memorial entry.

Section 5

Modes — what winning means

A mode is a ruleset: one per match. Elimination always wins in parallel — objectives are a second way to win, never a replacement.

Skirmish

The classic. Two squads, one map, last herd standing. Yours to configure: map, faction, squad, enemy, rules.

King of the Hill

Every map has a heart — a wreck, a track bed, a town square. Stand in it, alone, at the end of a round to bank a hold; first to five holds wins. Both teams in the ring is CONTESTED and banks nothing, which means the last pig to act in a round can walk in and deny the tick.

Countdown

One side arms the warhead; the other disarms it, shelters behind the bunker door, or solves the problem at the source. Five endings, all of them somebody's fault.

Steel Sow Raid

The boss mission. A colossal walking factory with three weak points at three heights — leg, coolant, sensors, in any order — then the head opens and the pilot answers for all of it. Bring the squad; leave with an anecdote.

Large Battle

Eight a side, one of every class, big maps only. No director, no gimmick — the mode is the scale.

Horde

Round-based survival against something the War Office declines to name in print. Each round fields more of them. Score is the deepest round you survive. Unlocked by... an event.

Basic Training

The tutorial. The range instructor takes full credit for everything you learn and no responsibility for how you use it.

Director Mode

The war plays itself on a clean, HUD-free screen — a cinematic camera watching two AI sides go at it. Excellent with a beverage.

Section 6

Mutators — how the match is fought

A mutator changes how you fight without changing what winning means. Stack as many as the compatibility board allows — your loadout shows as badges on deploy and again on the results screen.

Sudden Death

After turn 20, healing stops for good and every pig on the field caps at half health. A deadline, not a nerf.

Sub-toggle — CLOSING RING: the standable map shrinks every round. Outside the ring counts as a drowning, with the same paperwork.

Big Squads

Up to six a side. The extra spawn points are derived side-fairly and only ever on safe footing — a short squad is a fair match; a pig spawned in the river is not.

One Life

Every spare life stripped — the Dogs' second included. A Medic can still patch you up; nobody can bring you back. Absolute stakes, no exceptions, because a mutator called ONE LIFE with an asterisk would be a worse mutator.

Fog of War

Per-team visibility, class-varied sight — the Sniper sees far, the Phantom hears through walls. Unseen enemies are not drawn at all; you get a ghost marker at the last place you had eyes on, fading over three turns.

The honest part: the AI obeys exactly the same rules you do. It shoots at pigs it can see, walks toward last-known positions when the trail goes cold, and never gets a free peek. There is a ledger in the code that proves it.

Section 7

The Factions

Same pink hide, very different attitudes. Every pig on the field carries a unique handcrafted name in its faction's voice — and within one battle, no two pigs ever share one.

Olive drab · the default squad

Bacon Brigade

Salt-of-the-earth foot soldiers with polite British ranks. Dependable, disciplined, first over the top — grumbling the whole way, going anyway.

Sandy tan · desert camo

Desert Hams

Sun-baked sharpshooters named for heat, dune and mirage. Masters of the long game and the longer wait.

Navy blue · the naval set

Blue Boars

Salt-water swine with naval slang and a chip on the shoulder. Came ashore looking for a fight; never quite went back to the boats.

Crimson · the rivals

Red Rashers

The pantomime villains of the pork war. Loud, red, gloriously unsubtle — enthusiasm with explosives. Watch your flanks.

Clan epithets

The Tribals

The sixth faction. Bows, spears, invented clan names, and the only true arcs in the game. High ground was always theirs.

Kennel names · unit designations

The Dogs

The fifth team. A monoclass swarm, cyber-grafted, spine-gunned, and issued a spare life each. Diplomacy was never on the table.

Section 8

Operations — the campaign as a ladder

An Operation is a nine-rung tower climbed with one roster. Losses are permanent. The top rung does not introduce itself.

Your pigs carry service records between battles — kills, tours, medals, the works. A pig that falls on rung six is not on rung seven: the roster thins, the survivors harden, and the memorial board in the hangar remembers everyone who didn't come back, by name.

Operation I is FIRST FURROW. Summiting it earns an achievement; summiting it with zero pigs killed in action earns a different one, and the armband that comes with it is sewn on, not handed out.

Permadeath is the hook, not the rule: outside Operations — skirmish, King of the Hill, all of it — your pigs respawn like the professionals they pretend to be. Only the ladder keeps score forever.

Section 9

Between Tours

Home is a walkable hangar. The crew is there; the horizon is not quiet.

Between battles you have the run of the place: an outfitters for hats and loadouts, squad presets, daily contracts, a rank to climb from the sty up to Trench Sgt. Major, a medal wall with ladders deep enough to outlast you, and a Play of the Match when you earn one. Your veterans hang around the hangar between tours — you can rename any pig to the one word you will actually shout at it. Hamlet. Chops. Boris. Unit-7.

And on one wall, the memorial board. Every name on it is real, in the sense that matters: you fielded them, you named them, and you remember which rung it was.
